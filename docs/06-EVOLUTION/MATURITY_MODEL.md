# Maturity Model

> Defines the institutional framework used to measure the maturity, readiness and resilience of the Breto's Holding Group ecosystem.

---

# Purpose

The Maturity Model establishes a standardized framework for evaluating the ability of the BHG ecosystem to operate, evolve and scale sustainably.

Its purpose is to distinguish:

- existence from capability;
- activity from maturity;
- growth from readiness;
- complexity from institutional strength.

Maturity provides evidence for determining whether the ecosystem is prepared for increased responsibility, complexity or scale.

---

# Definition of Institutional Maturity

Institutional maturity represents the degree to which the BHG ecosystem can consistently perform its required responsibilities while preserving:

- governance integrity;
- architectural coherence;
- operational reliability;
- institutional knowledge;
- strategic continuity.

Maturity is demonstrated through evidence.

It is not determined solely by:

- age;
- size;
- revenue;
- repository count;
- number of employees;
- technological sophistication.

---

# Maturity Philosophy

BHG shall evaluate maturity as a multidimensional capability.

A mature ecosystem is not simply larger.

It is better able to:

- make sound decisions;
- preserve institutional knowledge;
- manage complexity;
- absorb change;
- maintain governance;
- create sustainable value.

---

# Maturity Principles

## Principle 1 — Evidence Before Classification

An ecosystem component shall not be considered mature merely because it declares maturity.

Maturity classification requires supporting evidence.

---

## Principle 2 — Capability Before Scale

A component should demonstrate sufficient capability before receiving greater operational or strategic responsibility.

---

## Principle 3 — Maturity Is Multidimensional

No single metric shall determine institutional maturity.

Assessment shall consider multiple dimensions.

---

## Principle 4 — Maturity Is Progressive

Maturity develops through measurable progression.

A component may improve, stagnate, regress or require remediation.

---

## Principle 5 — Maturity Must Be Observable

Maturity indicators shall be sufficiently explicit to permit:

- human evaluation;
- historical comparison;
- automated analysis.

---

# Maturity Dimensions

The BHG ecosystem shall evaluate maturity across multiple dimensions.

---

# 1. Institutional Maturity

Measures the strength of:

- purpose;
- identity;
- continuity;
- institutional responsibility.

Indicators may include:

- documented institutional purpose;
- stable foundational principles;
- succession readiness;
- historical preservation.

---

# 2. Governance Maturity

Measures the ability to maintain:

- clear authority;
- accountable decisions;
- documented governance;
- compliance;
- traceability.

Indicators may include:

- authority clarity;
- policy coverage;
- decision traceability;
- auditability;
- governance compliance.

---

# 3. Architectural Maturity

Measures the quality and stability of ecosystem structure.

Indicators may include:

- clear boundaries;
- explicit dependencies;
- source-of-truth integrity;
- modularity;
- controlled complexity.

Architectural maturity shall explicitly consider dependency integrity and architecture drift.

---

# 4. Operational Maturity

Measures the ability to execute reliably.

Indicators may include:

- defined processes;
- responsible ownership;
- operational continuity;
- quality management;
- incident management.

---

# 5. Technological Maturity

Measures the ability to develop and operate technology sustainably.

Indicators may include:

- system reliability;
- security;
- maintainability;
- scalability;
- technical governance.

Technology maturity shall not be confused with the use of advanced technology.

---

# 6. Knowledge Maturity

Measures the ability to create, preserve and reuse institutional knowledge.

Indicators may include:

- documentation quality;
- knowledge accessibility;
- historical preservation;
- reusable methodologies;
- learning systems.

---

# 7. Financial Maturity

Measures the organization's ability to sustain responsible economic activity.

Indicators may include:

- financial planning;
- capital discipline;
- risk management;
- resource allocation;
- sustainability.

Financial maturity is evaluated according to applicable governance and legal requirements.

---

# 8. Organizational Maturity

Measures the strength of organizational structures and capabilities.

Indicators may include:

- leadership depth;
- accountability;
- organizational clarity;
- capability ownership;
- succession readiness.

---

# 9. Innovation Maturity

Measures the ability to innovate without introducing uncontrolled institutional risk.

Indicators may include:

- experimentation capability;
- research processes;
- innovation governance;
- learning from experimentation;
- responsible technology adoption.

---

# 10. Ecosystem Maturity

Measures the ability of different BHG components to operate as a coherent system.

Indicators may include:

- repository integration;
- shared asset management;
- source-of-truth consistency;
- cross-repository synchronization;
- ecosystem-wide governance alignment.

---

# Maturity Assessment Principle

Maturity assessments shall evaluate both:

Current Capability
        +
Institutional Reliability
        +
Readiness for Increased Complexity

A component that performs successfully under limited complexity may not yet be mature enough to operate under significantly greater institutional demands.
Maturity Assessment Scope
The Maturity Model may be applied to:
BHG as a whole;
individual repositories;
business domains;
organizational entities;
capabilities;
technology platforms;
governance systems.
The assessment scope shall always be explicitly defined.


---

# Maturity Levels

The BHG ecosystem shall use a progressive maturity scale.

Each level represents an increasing degree of institutional capability, reliability and readiness.

---

## Level 0 — Undefined

The component exists or is proposed but has insufficient evidence to determine its maturity.

Characteristics:

- purpose may be unclear;
- ownership may be incomplete;
- governance may be undefined;
- architecture may be undocumented.

A component at Level 0 shall not be considered institutionally mature.

---

## Level 1 — Defined

The component has documented its basic identity and purpose.

Characteristics:

- purpose documented;
- ownership identified;
- basic structure established;
- applicable governance identified;
- initial documentation available.

Level 1 establishes institutional visibility.

It does not demonstrate operational maturity.

---

## Level 2 — Established

The component has formal structures and repeatable practices.

Characteristics:

- responsibilities defined;
- governance relationships established;
- dependencies documented;
- operational processes identified;
- core documentation maintained.

Level 2 demonstrates that the component has moved beyond initial definition.

---

## Level 3 — Operational

The component consistently performs its intended responsibilities.

Characteristics:

- processes operate reliably;
- ownership is active;
- governance controls are functioning;
- measurable outputs exist;
- dependencies are managed.

Level 3 represents operational maturity.

---

## Level 4 — Scalable

The component can support increased complexity and broader responsibility without proportional loss of reliability.

Characteristics:

- repeatable operations;
- reusable capabilities;
- controlled complexity;
- resilient architecture;
- measurable performance.

Level 4 indicates readiness for significant expansion.

---

## Level 5 — Institutionalized

The component has become a durable institutional capability.

Characteristics:

- long-term strategic relevance;
- stable ownership;
- strong governance;
- preserved institutional knowledge;
- resilient operations;
- intergenerational continuity.

Level 5 represents the highest maturity classification defined by this model.

---

# Maturity Transition Rules

Progression between maturity levels shall require evidence.

A component shall not advance solely because a roadmap milestone has been completed.

Advancement should demonstrate that the capabilities associated with the target level are operating reliably.

---

# Level Advancement Model

The general progression is:

Undefined

    ↓

Defined

    ↓

Established

    ↓

Operational

    ↓

Scalable

    ↓

Institutionalized

A component may remain at its current level when evidence is insufficient for progression.
Maturity Regression
Maturity is not permanent.
A component may regress when:
critical capabilities deteriorate;
governance controls fail;
ownership becomes unclear;
architecture becomes unstable;
operational reliability decreases;
institutional knowledge is lost.
Regression shall be documented and evaluated.

Evidence Requirements
Maturity assessments shall prioritize evidence such as:
approved documentation;
operational records;
audit results;

measurable performance;
historical evidence;

Self-declared maturity without supporting evidence shall not be sufficient.
Assessment Outcomes
An assessment may produce one of the following outcomes:

Maturing

Insufficient Evidence

Requires Remediation

Not Ready for Expansion
These outcomes complement the maturity level.
They do not replace it.
Maturity and Growth Relationship
Growth shall be evaluated against maturity.
The relationship is:
Growth

    +

Sufficient Maturity

    =

Sustainable Expansion
Growth that exceeds maturity capacity may increase:
operational risk;
governance burden;
architectural complexity;
financial exposure;
dependency concentration.
Readiness for Expansion
Before significant expansion, BHG should evaluate whether the affected component has sufficient maturity for the intended change.
Examples:
A repository at:
Level 1 — Defined
may be suitable for controlled development.
A repository at:
Level 4 — Scalable
may be suitable for broader institutional responsibility.
The appropriate threshold depends on the nature and risk of the expansion.
Maturity Gates
Major transitions may require maturity gates.
A maturity gate verifies:
required capabilities;
governance readiness;
architecture readiness;
operational readiness;
risk conditions.
A failed maturity gate does not automatically terminate an initiative.
It indicates that prerequisites require remediation or explicit governance acceptance.
Maturity and Roadmaps
Roadmaps shall identify the maturity required to achieve major objectives when appropriate.
A roadmap milestone may therefore specify:
maturity_requirement:

  minimum_level:

  required_dimensions:

  evidence_required:

  validation_method:
This allows strategic planning to remain connected to measurable institutional readiness.
Maturity and Capabilities
Capabilities shall have their own maturity trajectory.
For example:
Capability Defined

↓

Capability Established

↓

Capability Operational

↓

Capability Scalable

↓

Capability Institutionalized
Capability maturity shall contribute to overall organizational maturity.
Maturity and Repository Lifecycle
Repository lifecycle and maturity are related but distinct.
Lifecycle describes:
The stage of existence of a repository.
Maturity describes:
The capability and reliability demonstrated by that repository.
For example:
Lifecycle:
Operational Maturity

Maturity:
Level 2 — Established
or:
Lifecycle:
Active Development

Maturity:
Level 3 — Operational
Lifecycle state and maturity level shall therefore remain independently maintained.
Maturity Review Frequency
Assessment frequency shall depend on:
strategic importance;
change rate;
risk;
lifecycle stage;
governance requirements.
Foundational and ecosystem-critical components should receive more frequent formal review than low-risk experimental components.
Historical Maturity Tracking
Maturity assessments shall preserve historical progression.
Historical records should allow BHG to determine:
previous maturity level;
assessment date;
evidence used;
identified gaps;
remediation performed;
resulting maturity change.
Maturity history is institutional knowledge.

# GGC Maturity Restrictions

The GGC shall not:

- assign final institutional maturity without authorized assessment;
- approve maturity transitions;
- declare an ecosystem ready for expansion;
- override governance decisions;
- replace human assessment responsibility.

The GGC provides evidence and analytical support.

Human governance retains final authority.

BHG may assess maturity at the ecosystem level.

An ecosystem-level assessment shall consider:

- institutional maturity;
- governance maturity;
- architectural maturity;
- operational maturity;
- technological maturity;
- knowledge maturity;
- financial maturity;
- organizational maturity;
- innovation maturity;
- ecosystem maturity.

---

# Ecosystem Maturity Principle

The maturity of the entire ecosystem shall not be determined solely by its strongest components.

Critical weaknesses may constrain overall ecosystem readiness.

Conceptually:

Strong Components
        +
Critical Weakness
        =
Limited Ecosystem Readiness

Therefore ecosystem maturity assessments shall identify critical dependencies and bottlenecks.


---

Maturity Bottlenecks

A maturity bottleneck exists when one or more dimensions limit the ability of the ecosystem to safely advance.

Examples include:

insufficient governance capacity;

weak architecture;

inadequate financial resilience;

missing critical capabilities;

poor documentation;

unstable dependencies.


Bottlenecks shall be prioritized for remediation.


---

Expansion Readiness

Expansion readiness shall be evaluated using:

Current Maturity
        +
Required Capability
        +
Governance Readiness
        +
Operational Readiness
        +
Risk Acceptability

A component may be mature in general but not ready for a specific expansion.

Readiness is therefore contextual.


---

Maturity Remediation

When a maturity assessment identifies gaps, BHG may establish a remediation plan.

A remediation plan may define:

identified gap;

target maturity;

required capability;

corrective action;

responsible owner;

target review;

evidence requirement.


Remediation progress shall remain traceable.


---

Maturity Certification

Formal maturity certification may be introduced for ecosystem-critical components.

Certification shall require:

defined assessment scope;

evidence review;

authorized assessment;

documented decision;

historical record.


Certification shall never be inferred solely from maturity metrics.


---

Maturity Security and Integrity

Maturity records represent institutional assessment data.

They shall be protected through:

version control;

access control;

audit history;

change traceability;

integrity verification.


Unauthorized changes to maturity assessments shall be treated as governance violations.


---

Maturity Evolution

The Maturity Model shall evolve as BHG gains operational experience.

Future revisions may introduce:

domain-specific maturity models;

capability-specific maturity models;

quantitative benchmarks;

automated evidence collection;

predictive maturity analysis.


Evolution shall preserve historical compatibility and assessment traceability.


---

Maturity Compliance

An assessment shall be considered compliant when:

assessment scope is defined;

maturity dimensions are identified;

evidence is documented;

assessor responsibility is known;

maturity level is justified;

historical records are preserved.



---

Non-Compliance Conditions

The following conditions represent maturity assessment non-compliance:

unsupported maturity claims;

missing assessment evidence;

undefined assessment scope;

undocumented maturity transitions;

contradictory maturity records;

unauthorized maturity changes.


Non-compliant assessments shall enter remediation or reassessment.
